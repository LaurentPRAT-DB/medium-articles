AI is in its dynamo phase

The dynamo didn't make factories faster. Redesigning them did.

6 min read

Walk into a factory in 1890 and look up. The ceiling is a maze of spinning shafts, pulleys and leather belts, all driven by a single engine somewhere in the building. Every machine on the floor is tied to that engine by a belt, which means every machine has to stand where a belt can reach it.

Then electricity arrived, and for a long time almost nothing happened. In 1990 the Stanford economist Paul David explained why in a short paper called "The Dynamo and the Computer." Factories had swapped the steam engine for an electric dynamo and kept everything else: the same shafts, the same belts, the same floor plan.

The fix was not a better motor

The gains came when factories gave each machine its own motor. With no shafts overhead, buildings could have high ceilings, plenty of light and a safer floor. Machines could be arranged along the flow of materials instead of along the line of a shaft. Formlabs, writing about the history, argues that the modern factory emerged from exactly this shift to individually powered machines.

Productivity followed. US manufacturing productivity grew by more than five percent a year in the 1920s, roughly four decades after electricity became commercial, according to Slate's Undercover Economist column on David's work.

It took four decades

The delay was not a mystery once you look at the economics. Electric motors made up less than five percent of mechanical-drive capacity in US factories in 1899, and it took about two more decades to reach half. By one estimate, nearly forty years passed before electrification showed up in the productivity numbers.

Part of the reason is that the first step was easy. Bolting a motor onto an existing belt system needed no new building and no new workflow. The real fix meant new factories, new floor plans and a new way of organizing the work. David also argued that the First World War helped force the issue: immigration controls cut off a supply of cheap, untrained labor, and owners had to find another way.

The same pattern, a century later

Replace the factory with a company and the dynamo with AI, and the story starts to look familiar.

  • Steam engine swapped for a dynamo — A copilot or chatbot added to an existing process
  • Line shafts and belts — Approvals, handoffs, reviews and org charts built around human throughput
  • Machines placed where the belts reach — Teams and systems organized around where people sit
  • Unit drive: a motor per machine — AI embedded at each step, with the process rebuilt around it

The numbers fit the picture. McKinsey's 2025 survey of nearly 2,000 organizations found that 88 percent use AI in at least one function, but only 39 percent report any impact on earnings, and most of those say it is under five percent of EBIT. About six percent qualify as "high performers," where AI drives five percent or more of EBIT. The 2026 update looks much the same: 37 percent report a positive effect and the high-performer share has not moved.

What separates the six percent is not the model they use or the size of the budget. High performers are nearly three times as likely to have fundamentally redesigned their workflows. In the 2026 survey, roughly three-quarters of them redesign work around AI, compared with about a quarter of everyone else.

These are survey correlations, not proof of cause, and several of the secondary sources are consultancy and vendor writing. Treat the exact figures with some caution. The direction is consistent across sources.

Why the bill looks big and the impact looks small

If AI speeds up one step, such as drafting, summarizing or coding, the bottleneck simply moves. The draft arrives sooner, then waits in the same review queue, the same approval chain and the same overnight batch as before. The time saved in one place is lost in the next.

There is a second leak. Faster cycle times can be eaten by per-use costs before they ever reach earnings, so a team can feel quicker while the finance numbers stay flat. That is the dynamo on a belt system: the motor works, and the factory around it does not let it matter.

In the bolted-on version, AI summarizes the claim file for the adjuster. That is useful, but verification is still manual, claims still wait for sign-off, and payment still runs in a batch. The end-to-end time barely moves.

In the redesigned version, verification happens as the claim arrives: the system checks policy cover, validates documents and photos, and scores fraud signals. The decision is automatic within set limits, such as claim type, amount and fraud score. Payment is triggered by the decision itself, with no queue and no overnight batch. Adjusters stop touching every claim and spend their time on the complex and flagged ones, which is where their judgment is worth the most.

What to change

The lesson from the factory is that the new power source was never the hard part. These are the changes I would make first.

  • Start from the outcome, not the tool. Pick a whole process and ask how it would look if AI handled most of the steps from the start.
  • Let AI own steps. An assistant that helps one person is a motor on a belt. An agent that completes a step and escalates only the edge cases is the unit drive.
  • Fix the wiring. Clean data, clear permissions and working integrations are what let AI reach the work. Without them it stays stuck at the shaft.
  • Measure the workflow, not the tool. Track cycle time, straight-through rate, escalation rate, quality and cost per transaction. License counts and prompt volumes say little.
  • Design the guardrails. Automated decisions need clear limits, logging and an explanation for each outcome. Regulation may require human review for some decisions, so the "within limits" line is a design choice and not only a technical one.
  • Aim beyond efficiency. About 80 percent of respondents in McKinsey's survey set efficiency as the goal, while high performers also pursue growth and new offerings.

The technology arrived long before the organizations did, and the same thing is happening again. The useful question is not which AI tool to add. It is which belts are still hanging from your ceiling.
